\section{Introduction}

Deep learning has become a central approach within machine learning due to its ability to learn hierarchical representations directly from raw data, reducing the dependence on manually engineered features \cite{b1}, \cite{b2}. By combining multiple nonlinear processing layers, deep neural networks can progressively transform low-level input patterns into increasingly abstract representations, which has made them particularly effective in domains involving high-dimensional and structured data, such as images, speech, natural language, and temporal sequences \cite{b2}, \cite{b3}. This capability is especially relevant when the underlying data contains spatial or temporal relationships that are difficult to describe through handcrafted rules \cite{b1}, \cite{b4}.

Convolutional Neural Networks (CNNs) are among the most influential deep learning architectures for image recognition and classification \cite{b3}, \cite{b4}. Their effectiveness derives from the use of learnable convolutional filters, local connectivity, and weight sharing, allowing the network to exploit spatial structure while using fewer parameters than equivalent fully connected architectures \cite{b1}, \cite{b4}. Through successive convolutional, activation, pooling, and classification layers, CNNs can learn increasingly complex feature maps, from low-level patterns such as edges and textures to higher-level object-specific representations \cite{b4}, \cite{b5}. The importance of CNNs became especially evident after the success of deep convolutional architectures in large-scale visual recognition benchmarks, where deeper models, GPU-based training, ReLU activations, dropout, and data augmentation contributed to major improvements in image classification performance \cite{b3}. These properties make CNNs suitable for visual classification tasks in which the objective is not only to obtain high predictive performance, but also to evaluate how architectural choices such as filter size, number of filters, stride, padding, pooling strategy, and network depth affect generalization.

In image-based deep learning problems, generalization is strongly influenced by the amount, diversity, and representativeness of the training data \cite{b6}. Data augmentation addresses this limitation by applying label-preserving transformations that increase the variability of the training set without requiring the collection of new annotated samples \cite{b6}. In computer vision, common augmentation strategies include geometric transformations, such as rotation, translation, scaling, cropping, and flipping, as well as photometric transformations, such as brightness, contrast, saturation, blur, and color adjustments \cite{b6}. These transformations can improve robustness to realistic visual variations and reduce overfitting, particularly when the training dataset does not fully cover the expected distribution of test examples \cite{b3}, \cite{b6}. Transfer learning and fine-tuning provide an additional strategy for improving generalization by reusing models pretrained on large datasets and adapting their learned representations to a new, related task \cite{b6}, \cite{b7}.

Recurrent Neural Networks (RNNs), in contrast, are designed for sequential data, where the order of observations carries predictive information \cite{b2}, \cite{b8}. Unlike standard feedforward models, which process fixed-size inputs independently, RNNs maintain a hidden state that evolves over time and allows previous observations to influence later predictions \cite{b8}. This makes recurrent architectures relevant for tasks involving time-dependent patterns, including speech recognition, language modeling, sequence prediction, and time series forecasting \cite{b8}. However, conventional recurrent architectures may struggle to learn long-range temporal dependencies due to vanishing and exploding gradients during backpropagation through time \cite{b9}. Long Short-Term Memory (LSTM) networks were introduced to address this limitation through gated memory mechanisms that regulate the storage, update, and retrieval of information across time steps, enabling more stable learning over longer temporal intervals \cite{b9}. For this reason, LSTMs are commonly used when the predictive target may depend on information distributed across different temporal horizons.

This work investigates these two families of architectures through two complementary experimental problems defined in the practical work statement 
\cite{b10}. The first task concerns fruit image classification using the Fruits-360 dataset, composed of standardized RGB images of fruit categories captured on a uniform white background \cite{b10}, \cite{b11}. The objective is to design, train, and evaluate CNN-based models while systematically analyzing the impact of different architectural and training choices, including convolutional filter configurations, stride and padding strategies, pooling methods, regularization, data augmentation, and transfer learning using pretrained models such as ResNet50 and MobileNetV2 \cite{b3}, \cite{b6}, \cite{b7}, \cite{b10}. The goal is not only to obtain high classification performance, but also to interpret how each design decision affects accuracy, precision, recall, F1-score, overfitting behavior, and class-level performance \cite{b10}.

The second task addresses 24-hour ahead temperature forecasting using the Jena Climate dataset, a multivariate meteorological time series recorded at 10-minute intervals between 2009 and 2016 \cite{b10}. This problem is used to evaluate the ability of recurrent models to capture temporal dependencies in weather-related variables such as temperature, pressure, humidity, wind speed, wind direction, air density, and solar radiation \cite{b10}. Simple RNN and LSTM models are compared under different input time-window configurations in order to assess how recurrent architecture and temporal context influence forecasting accuracy, robustness, and error behavior \cite{b8}, \cite{b10}. Model performance is evaluated using regression metrics, including the required Root Mean Squared Logarithmic Error (RMSLE), complemented where appropriate by additional error measures such as Mean Absolute Error (MAE) and Root Mean Squared Error (RMSE) \cite{b10}.

The main motivation of this project is to develop an empirical and comparative understanding of how deep learning model design, preprocessing decisions, and validation strategies affect performance across two representative problem classes: image classification and time series forecasting. The adopted methodology includes data preprocessing and normalization, exploratory data analysis, incremental model design, hyperparameter tuning, validation, training curve inspection, metric-based evaluation, overfitting analysis, and comparison between baseline, improved, and pretrained architectures \cite{b10}. Rather than focusing exclusively on a single final model, this work emphasizes experimental comparison and interpretation, aiming to justify the selected techniques and explain the observed results in relation to the architectural and training decisions tested.

The remainder of this paper is organized as follows. Section II presents the related work and theoretical background on CNNs, data augmentation, RNNs, and LSTMs. Section III details the followed work methodology. Section IV presents and discusses the results obtained for both tasks, including metric comparisons, learning curves, convolutional layer visualizations, best- and worst-case examples, and error analysis. Finally, Section V summarizes the main conclusions and outlines possible directions for future improvement.

\subsection{CNN Task}
 
\subsubsection{Dataset and Preprocessing}
The Fruits-360 dataset was used for the image classification task. The selected version includes RGB images of 142 classes spanning fruits, vegetables, nuts, and seeds, organized into predefined Training (50\%), Validation (25\%), and Test (25\%) directories, one subfolder per class. This original split was preserved to avoid data leakage. Since the selected version includes non-fruit categories, the task was treated as a general multi-class object classification problem over all available classes rather than a fruit-only subset.
Two image-size strategies were adopted depending on model type: 100x100 for the custom CNN, to enable efficient experimentation across architectural variants (filters, strides, padding, pooling, dropout, augmentation); and  larger image resolutions for pretrained models such as ResNet50 and MobileNetV2. This difference means the custom CNN and pretrained models are not compared under identical input conditions, since the 100×100 images contain less visual information. 

The preprocessing pipeline consisted of: loading images from the class-folder structure; resizing according to model type; converting all images to RGB for consistent three-channel input; normalizing pixel values from [0, 255] to [0, 1]; encoding class labels from folder names; and preserving strict separation between validation (used for monitoring and tuning) and test (used only for final evaluation) sets.

\subsubsection{Baseline Architecture}
The baseline CNN follows a hierarchical structure receiving 100×100×3 RGB images and processing them through three convolutional blocks. Each block consists of a Conv2D layer with 3×3 kernels and same padding, followed by batch normalization, ReLU activation, and MaxPooling2D. The number of filters increases progressively from 32 to 64 and 128, allowing the network to learn increasingly complex visual representations. After the convolutional stage, a GlobalAveragePooling2D layer aggregates the spatial feature maps, followed by a Dense layer with 128 units, Dropout (rate 0.3), and a final Dense layer with 142 units and softmax activation. The total number of trainable parameters is 128,302.
The model was compiled with the Adam optimizer at a learning rate of 0.0003, using sparse categorical cross-entropy loss and accuracy as the training metric. Two callbacks were used: EarlyStopping monitoring validation loss with patience of 5 epochs, and ReduceLROnPlateau reducing the learning rate by a factor of 0.3 with patience of 2 epochs.

\subsubsection{Training Strategy and Model Validation}

All CNN models were trained using the same optimization strategy to ensure fair comparison between experiments. Early Stopping was employed to monitor validation loss and restore the best model weights when no improvement was observed. Additionally, the ReduceLROnPlateau callback was used to automatically reduce the learning rate whenever validation performance stagnated.
The model is trained for a maximum of 5 epochs using the training dataset, while the validation dataset is used to monitor generalization performance after each epoch.

Model evaluation was performed on an independent test set using classification accuracy, precision, recall, and F1-score. These metrics were computed both globally and at the class level to assess model generalization and identify potential weaknesses in individual categories.

\subsubsection{Experiments}

A sequence of controlled experiments was conducted to investigate the influence of different architectural and preprocessing choices on classification performance.
\begin{itemize}

\item{Experiment 2 and 3 - Impact of the Number of Filters:} Two additional filter configurations were evaluated. The first reduced the network capacity by using 16–32–64 filters, while the second increased the model complexity using 64–128–256 filters.

\item{Experiment 4 - Impact of Valid Padding:} The baseline architecture employed \underline{same} padding in all convolutional layers. An alternative configuration using \underline{valid} padding was evaluated to investigate the impact of this change.

\item{Experiment 5 - Impact of Average Pooling:} To evaluate whether preserving the average response of a region is more or less effective than keeping only the strongest activation, \underline{max} pooling layers were replaced by \underline{average} pooling layers.

\item{Experiment 6 - Impact of Stride Variation:} The first convolutional layer was modified to use a stride of 2 instead of 1. The first max pooling layer is removed in this experiment to avoid reducing the image too aggressively.

\item{Experiment 7 - Impact of Data Augmentation:} To test whether exposing the model to slightly modified versions of the original images improves robustness and generalization, the baseline CNN was extended with  horizontal flipping, rotations, zooming, and translation operations applied to the training images.

\item{Experiment 8 - MobileNetV2 as a Frozen Feature Extractor:} Transfer learning was evaluated using MobileNetV2 pretrained on ImageNet as a frozen feature extractor. Images were resized to 224x224 pixels and preprocessed according to the network requirements. The pretrained base is frozen, meaning that its weights are not updated during training. Only the custom classification head is trained, composed of global average pooling, a dense layer, dropout, and a final softmax layer with one output unit per class.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{RNN Task}

\subsubsection{Dataset and Preprocessing} The Jena Climate dataset contains 420,551 observations recorded at 10-minute intervals between 2009 and 2016, containing 15 meteorological variables. Preprocessing followed several steps, each justified by data characteristics observed during exploratory analysis.

\begin{itemize}
\item Feature selection removed redundant derived variables (Tpot, Tdew, VPmax, VPact, VPdef, H2OC) identified through correlation analysis as highly collinear with the retained variables, reducing multicollinearity while preserving seven physically meaningful predictors: temperature, pressure, relative humidity, wind speed, air density, specific humidity, and wind direction.

\item Invalid sensor readings (wv = -9999) were treated as missing values and imputed via forward-fill.

\item Wind direction, being circular (0°-360°), was encoded as sine/cosine pairs to preserve its periodic nature. 

\item Cyclical representations of the hour of the day and the day of the year were introduced to provide the models with information about daily and seasonal patterns.

\item The 10-minute data was downsampled to hourly resolution via mean aggregation, reducing both data volume and measurement noise.

\item MinMax normalization to [0, 1] was fitted exclusively on the training set and applied to all splits, satisfying the non-negativity requirement of RMSLE.

\item Sliding windows of length (W) were generated, where each sequence was used to predict the temperature 24 hours ahead.

\item The dataset was split chronologically into 70\% training, 15\% validation, and 15\% test sets, without shuffling, to prevent temporal data leakage.


\end{itemize}

\bigbreak

A data leakage audit was conducted before training to ensure that no future information or validation/test data contaminated the training process. The checks verified that the scaler was fitted exclusively on the training set, the dataset split was strictly chronological with no overlap, the sequence generation procedure introduced no look-ahead bias, and the cyclical features were derived solely from timestamp information. Assertions were used to automatically validate these conditions whenever the notebook was executed.

\subsubsection{Baseline Performance} - The baseline CNN achieved a test accuracy of 96.07\% and a test loss of 0.1249, with macro precision, recall, and F1-score all approximately 0.96. Training accuracy increased from 42.83\% to 91.92\% across five epochs, while validation accuracy increased from 85.07\% to 96.12\%. Both training and validation losses decreased consistently, indicating no overfitting. The per-class report showed that some visually similar categories remained challenging for the baseline model, suggesting that the main difficulty was fine-grained discrimination between visually similar variants of the same fruit family, rather than general object recognition.

\subsubsection{Experiment 2 and 3} - Reducing the number of filters to 16-32-64 (Experiment 2) produced a test accuracy of 95.46\% with only 50,558 parameters, close to the baseline's 96.07\% despite using significantly fewer parameters, indicating that relatively lightweight architectures are sufficient to learn discriminative features from the Fruits-360 dataset. However, the per-class analysis showed that the smaller model struggled more with some visually similar categories.
In contrast, increasing the number of filters to 64-128-256 (Experiment 3) raised parameter count to 423,374 and reached 96.88\% validation accuracy by epoch 4, but validation loss spiked in epoch 5, dropping validation accuracy to 85.04\% and final test accuracy to 84.90\%, indicating that increasing model capacity did not translate into better generalization and instead introducing instability. suggesting the baseline configuration (32-64-128) offers the best trade-off between accuracy and stability.

\subsubsection{Experiment 4} - Switching from same to valid padding produced a test accuracy of 96.00\%, nearly identical to the baseline (96.07\%), with validation accuracy rising from 86.42\% to 95.96\% and stable loss curves. The per-class errors remained concentrated on the same visually similar categories observed in the baseline, suggesting that the padding strategy had little influence on the model's ability to discriminate between classes. Overall, same padding remained marginally preferable due to its slightly better performance and preservation of spatial information.

\subsubsection{Experiment 5} - Replacing max pooling with average pooling reduced test accuracy to 90.10\%, a substantial drop from the baseline's 96.07\% despite identical parameter counts, indicating that the observed performance difference was due to the pooling strategy rather than model capacity. The less stable validation behaviour and the increased number of problematic classes further suggest that average pooling reduced the model's ability to preserve the most discriminative features. Overall, the results demonstrate that max pooling is better suited for this dataset, providing higher accuracy and stronger class-level performance.

\subsubsection{Experiment 6} - The stride variation experiment achieved a test accuracy of 92.66\%, lower than the baseline's 96.07\%, despite having the same number of parameters. Increasing the stride in the first convolutional layer led to less stable validation performance and reduced the model's ability to distinguish between visually similar categories, indicating that for this dataset, preserving spatial resolution in the early layers appears to be more beneficial, while dimensionality reduction through max pooling after initial feature extraction remains a better strategy. 

\subsubsection{Experiment 7} - The data augmentation experiment resulted in a substantial decrease in performance, reducing test accuracy from 96.07\% to 72.78\%. Training accuracy still increased (39.45\% to 87.65\%), but validation accuracy peaked at only 77.27\% before declining to 72.77\%. Since the model architecture remained unchanged, this degradation can be attributed to the augmentation strategy itself. The applied transformations introduced variability that does not reflect the test distribution, making the learning task harder rather than improving generalization. This indicates that, for the Fruits-360 dataset, aggressive geometric augmentation is counterproductive.

\subsubsection{Experiment 8} - The MobileNetV2-based model achieved the best overall performance, reaching 100.00\% test accuracy with perfect macro precision, recall, and F1-score. This result shows the effectiveness of transfer learning as  the pretrained base was frozen and only the custom classification head was trained. Therefore, the model benefited from generic visual representations learned from a much larger dataset while adapting the final classifier to the 142 Fruits-360 classes. The larger 224×224 input size compared to the 100×100 used for the custom CNN experiments may have preserved more visual detail, making fine-grained class separation easier. However, this comparison should be interpreted cautiously, since MobileNetV2 has many more total parameters than the custom CNN and relies on pretrained knowledge from ImageNet. Despite these differences, transfer learning emerged as the most effective approach among all evaluated CNN configurations.
